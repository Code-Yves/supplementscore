How SupplementScore is made

SupplementScore is openly mostly AI-generated. The pages are drawn from the same credible sources doctors rely on: PubMed, the Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, the FDA, EFSA, EMA, and Health Canada.

Who writes the pages

Language models draft the supplement cards, the articles, the comparisons, and the condition pages. A person sets the scoring rules, decides what is published, and corrects mistakes readers flag. The same public sources named above are the raw material: peer-reviewed papers and regulator documents, not ads and not affiliate reviews.

The site is bringing physicians on board. No physician signs a page before it is published, and a page does not carry a medical-review label. This is an educational reference. It is not personal medical advice. Scoring rules live on the methodology page.

Study count: 3,033

One study is one PubMed ID. The same ID on many pages counts once.

Method. Read every .html, .js, and .json file in the site repository, except node_modules, tests, scripts, outputs, _archive, reviews, _mockups, og, dist, _docs, fr, es, and any filename containing “mockup”. Keep an ID when the text has PMID followed by a 6- to 9-digit number, or a pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ link with that number. That scan found 3,033 distinct IDs. The procedure is scripts/transparency_counts.py in the public repository.

Human research hours: 1,505,381,664

This is a lower-bound estimate of time people spent inside the cited human studies. It is participant-time. It is not hours worked by SupplementScore staff, and it is not time a physician spent on these pages.

Method. Use the HTML pages from the same file set, plus data.js (the supplement text). A sentence counts only when it has all three of these, and no extras:

  • Exactly one PubMed ID.
  • Exactly one headcount: a number followed by participants, subjects, patients, volunteers, individuals, people, adults, women, men, children, or infants. Headcounts under 8 or over 100,000 are skipped.
  • Exactly one follow-up: “for”, “over”, “across”, “during”, “lasting”, “lasted”, “followed for”, “follow-up of”, or “median of” (including “a median of” and “mean of”), plus a number of days, weeks, months, or years. A form such as “12-week trial” counts too. Follow-up shorter than 7 days, or longer than 25 years, is skipped.

A range such as 3–5 years uses the midpoint. Hours use 24 hours in a day, 168 in a week, 730.5 in a month (365.25 ÷ 12 days), and 8,766 in a year (365.25 days). People times those hours is the contribution. Each PubMed ID is kept once. If two sentences disagree, the count is rejected instead of averaged. Sentences that omit a headcount or a follow-up are left out. They are not guessed.

That rule matched 24 of the 3,033 studies. Their participant-hours sum to 1,505,381,664. One of them is the Physicians' Health Study II, PubMed ID 23117775. The page states a follow-up of 11 years in 14,641 men, which is 1,411,773,066 of those hours. The other 23 studies make up the remaining 93,608,598 hours.

Most cited studies never state both a headcount and a follow-up in the same sentence as a single PubMed ID. Those studies are in the study count. They are not in the hour total. The hour total is a floor, not a census of every trial behind the site.

Money and corrections

There are no ads, no sponsors, and no affiliate links. Who pays for hosting is in About — Transparency. To flag a wrong dose, study, or safety line, use Flag an error or write to editorial@supplementscore.org with the page, the sentence, and a public source.
