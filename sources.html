Peer-reviewed databases

PubMed ↗
Primary citation source
NCBI’s 35-million-citation biomedical database. Every supplement’s evidence base is checked against PubMed via the eUtils API. Citation counts on supplement pages are live-fetched here.
Cochrane Library ↗
Systematic reviews
Gold-standard meta-analyses. When a Cochrane review exists for a supplement, it is treated as the primary evidence anchor regardless of how many individual RCTs disagree.
Examine.com ↗
Supplement research
Independent, ad-free supplement evidence database. Used for cross-checking effect sizes and identifying recently-published studies.
Natural Medicines (TRC) ↗
Drug interactions
Therapeutic Research Center’s clinical reference for natural products. Primary source for the supplement-drug interaction database.

National regulators

FDA ↗
United States
Recall notices, warning letters, MedWatch adverse event reports, and authorized health claims (e.g. psyllium, oat beta-glucan). Reviewed weekly.
EMA ↗
European Union
European Medicines Agency. Source for EU-specific safety reviews, herbal monograph database, and pharmacovigilance signals.
EFSA ↗
EU food safety
European Food Safety Authority. Source for EU health claims, novel-food approvals, and tolerable upper intake levels.
MHRA ↗
United Kingdom
Yellow Card scheme reports and herbal medicines registration scheme. Cross-referenced against FDA and EMA actions.
BfArM ↗
Germany
German federal regulator. Source for several Tier-4 classifications where Germany has acted ahead of FDA (e.g. greater celandine, kava withdrawals).
TGA ↗
Australia
Therapeutic Goods Administration. Australia regulates supplements more strictly than the US; TGA actions are an early signal for new safety concerns.
Health Canada ↗
Canada
Natural Health Products Database — license numbers and authorized claims for ~75,000 products marketed in Canada.
CDC ↗
US public health
MMWR and poison center surveillance data. Cited for outbreak reports (e.g. kava poisoning calls, kratom co-use trends).

Research institutions

NIH ↗
United States
NIH Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheets, NCCIH clinical trial database, and NHANES population intake data.
WHO ↗
Global
World Health Organization monographs on selected medicinal plants and the Essential Medicines List (relevant for ORS, vitamin A, iron, etc.).
Harvard, Johns Hopkins, Stanford, Karolinska
Academic medicine
Translational research, professional society guidelines, and editorial commentary used to contextualize trial findings.
The Lancet · NEJM · BMJ · JAMA
Top-tier journals
Primary RCTs and meta-analyses cited throughout the database. Recent meta-analyses in these journals trigger automatic re-review.

What we explicitly do NOT use

Manufacturer literature, affiliate-driven supplement review sites, nutritional industry trade publications, celebrity-endorsed product pages, and retailer product pages are explicitly excluded as primary sources. They may be cited in an article only as evidence of a marketing claim being analyzed.

See the methodology page for how these sources are weighted in the composite score, and the funder policy for how each cited paper is tagged with a funding source and conflict-of-interest flag.
