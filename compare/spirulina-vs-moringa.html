Spirulina vs Moringa — two "superfood" green powders compared honestly

Updated 2026-05-17 · Reviewed by SupplementScore editors · No sponsorships

Spirulina (a blue-green algae) and Moringa oleifera (the leaves of the moringa tree) are the two most popular "green superfood" powders. Both are nutrient-dense whole foods, and both have a marketing layer that runs well ahead of the evidence. Spirulina has more (and better-designed) human trial data — modest signals on lipids, blood pressure, and allergic rhinitis. Moringa has limited rigorous human evidence; most of its marketing relies on nutritional density (which is real) and animal data (which doesn't generalise well). Both have meaningful contamination risks that buyers should understand.

How they actually work

Spirulina — phycocyanin, gamma-linolenic acid, dense protein

Spirulina (Arthrospira platensis / maxima) is a filamentous cyanobacterium farmed in alkaline waters. It's roughly 60% protein by dry weight, dense in iron and B vitamins (but with the pseudo-B12 caveat below), and contains phycocyanin — a blue pigment with antioxidant and proposed anti-inflammatory activity in cell and animal studies. Typical supplemental doses are 1–8 g/day. Trial endpoints with the cleanest signals: modest lipid reductions, modest BP reductions, reductions in allergic rhinitis symptoms.

Moringa — nutritional density and weak human evidence

Moringa oleifera leaves are remarkably nutrient-dense when dried — gram for gram providing meaningful iron, calcium, vitamin C, vitamin A precursors, and protein. As a whole-food fortification in undernourished settings, this is genuinely valuable. As a supplement-pill addition to an already adequate diet, the evidence base is thin. Most "moringa lowers blood sugar" or "moringa lowers BP" claims rest on small, poorly-controlled human trials and rodent data; rigorous trials are limited.

The B12 problem

Spirulina is sometimes marketed as a B12 source for vegans. This is a misleading claim — spirulina B12 is largely "pseudo-B12" (cobalamin analogs that don't have the active B12 ring structure required for human metabolism, and may actually interfere with active B12 assay readings and metabolism). Vegans need methylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin, not spirulina. Moringa contains minimal B12.

Contamination — important for both

Spirulina: cyanobacteria can produce microcystins (hepatotoxins) under certain growth conditions. Reputable producers test and certify microcystin-free product. Heavy metal contamination (lead, mercury, arsenic) is also a real issue from contaminated water sources. Buy from manufacturers that publish third-party microcystin and heavy-metal testing (e.g., USP-verified or third-party-tested brands). Moringa: heavy metal uptake from soil is a documented issue — leaves accumulate lead and other metals from contaminated growing environments. Origin matters; reputable certified-organic sources with third-party testing are safer.

Iron content — real, but with caveats

Both powders have meaningful iron content per gram. However, plant iron is non-heme (lower bioavailability than animal iron) and is impacted by phytate and tannin content. For diagnosed iron-deficiency anemia, a specific iron supplement (ferrous bisglycinate every other day) is the better intervention. Spirulina/moringa as supportive food can help, but neither is appropriate as primary iron-deficiency treatment.

The "energy" claim

Both products are marketed for "energy" — typically a vague combination of "B vitamins + iron + chlorophyll." If you have iron-deficiency-related fatigue, treat the iron deficiency. If your "low energy" is sleep-, mood-, or thyroid-related, neither greens powder will solve it. Don't substitute greens powders for actual workup of fatigue.

Practical rule. If you specifically want a supplement with a small but real trial signal on lipids, BP, or allergic rhinitis: spirulina 2–4 g/day from a third-party-tested, microcystin-verified source. If you want a nutrient-dense food addition to an otherwise vegetable-light diet: moringa powder added to smoothies or oatmeal (treat it as a food fortification, not a supplement). For "I want one greens powder for general health": eat actual vegetables — they're cheaper and meta-analysed.

Dose, form, and timing

Spirulina: 1–8 g/day, divided. Tablets and powder forms. Take with food. Allergic rhinitis dose: 2 g/day. Lipid/BP dose: 1–4 g/day for 8–12 weeks.

Moringa: 1–3 teaspoons (~3–9 g) of leaf powder daily, added to food. Capsules at 400–800 mg/day are sub-therapeutic for any specific claim — most "indications" require 2–5 g/day of dried leaf in trials, and these trials are mostly small.

Safety and interactions

Spirulina: well-tolerated in clean product. Caution in phenylketonuria (high phenylalanine content). Theoretical autoimmune flare in patients with certain conditions (small case reports — not well established). Avoid in pregnancy without prescriber input (contamination risk concerns).

Moringa: well-tolerated as food. Theoretical caution with diabetes medications (mild glucose-lowering effect). Avoid moringa root and bark (contain abortifacient alkaloids). Pregnancy: avoid in supplemental doses; pregnancy-safe use limited to leaves as food and discussed with prescriber.

Cost

Spirulina powder/tablets: $0.20–0.60 per day at trial-cited doses. Moringa: $0.15–0.50 per day for whole leaf powder. Both are inexpensive but neither is a unique value proposition.

Who should pick each

Pick spirulina if: you want a supplement with modest cardiovascular evidence (lipids, BP), you have seasonal allergic rhinitis and want a non-drug adjunct, you can source a third-party-tested microcystin-verified product.

Pick moringa (as food) if: you want a high-nutrient-density food addition to smoothies/cooking, you're working with a population where dietary diversity is limited, you can source a reputably tested certified-organic product.

Pick neither if: you eat vegetables already, you want a B12 source (use methylcobalamin), you're using greens powders to compensate for an otherwise nutrient-poor diet without addressing the underlying eating pattern.

What we'd actually buy

For someone with mild dyslipidemia, seasonal allergic rhinitis, and a clean diet: spirulina 2 g/day from a third-party-tested source for 12 weeks; reassess. For someone wanting a food-fortification addition to a vegetable-light diet: moringa leaf powder 1 tsp/day in smoothies; treat as a food, not a supplement. For "I want to be healthier": eat vegetables; the trial base is enormous.
