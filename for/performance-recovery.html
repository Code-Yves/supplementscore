Supplements for performance recovery

Recovery is built on sleep, protein, and adequate energy intake. Supplements optimise the margins — they don't substitute.

"Recovery" supplements span an enormous range — from things that genuinely accelerate between-session recovery (adequate protein, creatine, omega-3 EPA/DHA at meaningful doses, tart cherry for soreness) to a much larger marketing layer of "recovery blends," BCAAs, and proprietary post-workout formulas with minimal independent benefit. The biggest determinants of recovery are sleep (7–9 hours, consistent timing), total daily protein intake (1.6–2.2 g/kg for athletes), and overall energy availability. Supplements are a small accelerator on top, not a substitute. This page covers the items with real RCT support and the items most worth skipping.

The recovery hierarchy — what actually matters in order

Step 1: Sleep

The single biggest recovery lever. 7–9 hours of consistent, dark, cool, screen-free sleep. Even one night of restricted sleep significantly impairs muscle protein synthesis and increases injury risk. No supplement substitutes for sleep. Magnesium glycinate 300 mg at bedtime is a reasonable backstop; melatonin 0.3–1 mg can help with travel/circadian disruption.

Step 2: Protein intake

Athletes need 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day of protein, distributed across 4–5 meals/snacks at ~0.3–0.4 g/kg per dose. The biggest "supplement" effect in recovery comes from hitting this number — and a whey or plant-protein blend post-workout is often the easiest way to do it. Casein or a slow-digesting protein before sleep adds a modest overnight protein synthesis benefit.

Step 3: Energy availability

Sustained energy deficit (relative energy deficiency in sport, RED-S) impairs recovery, hormones, bone density, and immune function — particularly in endurance athletes and in women. Pay attention to total caloric intake, not just protein.

Step 4: The high-evidence supplement layer

Creatine monohydrate 5 g/day — broad recovery and performance evidence base, including cognitive recovery under sleep deprivation. Omega-3 EPA+DHA 2–3 g/day — modest DOMS reduction and broader anti-inflammatory benefit. Vitamin D3 to a 25-OH-D target of 30–50 ng/mL. Magnesium glycinate 300 mg at night for sleep quality and cramps. Caffeine 3–6 mg/kg pre-session for performance; not a recovery agent.

Step 5: Conditional adjuncts

Tart cherry concentrate 30 mL or 480 mg powder daily for 5 days bracketing high-volume events — reduces DOMS and inflammatory markers (Bell 2014, Howatson 2010). Collagen peptides 15 g + 50 mg vitamin C 30–60 minutes before tendon/ligament-loading rehab work — for tendon collagen synthesis specifically (Shaw 2017). Citrulline malate 6–8 g pre-workout — reduces post-workout soreness in high-rep training.

Step 6: Specific situations

Heavy travel: melatonin 0.3–1 mg + magnesium glycinate at destination bedtime. Multi-day stage events: tart cherry pre/post each stage. Sleep-deprived training blocks: creatine especially helpful for cognitive recovery. Concurrent illness: zinc lozenges if early respiratory illness; address sleep and energy intake before adding more.

What to skip

  • BCAAs as a standalone recovery aid — if total daily protein is adequate, BCAAs add nothing. Whey is cheaper, more complete, and produces equivalent or better MPS.
  • "Recovery blends" with 20+ ingredients at sub-therapeutic doses — most ingredients are at fractions of trial-cited doses. Spend the same money on creatine + omega-3 + protein.
  • Glutamine for "muscle recovery" — the trial evidence in trained athletes is largely null. Glutamine may have a small role in gut barrier function in extreme endurance conditions; otherwise unnecessary.
  • Chronic high-dose NSAIDs (ibuprofen/naproxen) for post-workout soreness — masks pain at the cost of impaired muscle protein synthesis, impaired tendon collagen synthesis, and GI/cardiovascular risks. Short-term use only.
  • Cold water immersion / ice baths post-hypertrophy training — blunts long-term muscle adaptation. Reasonable for tournament recovery between same-day events; counterproductive after strength training.
  • Mega-dose antioxidants (vitamin C 1+ g, vitamin E 400+ IU) after training — blunts training adaptation by suppressing exercise-induced ROS signalling. Avoid for routine post-workout use.
  • "Hangover-style" recovery IVs and B-vitamin cocktails — typically unsupported by evidence; if you're dehydrated, water with electrolytes works.
  • CBD products marketed for recovery — limited and inconsistent trial evidence; product variability; potential drug-test concerns for tested athletes.
Educational reference, not medical advice. Athletes with chronic fatigue, frequent illness, mood changes, menstrual irregularities, or bone-stress injuries deserve evaluation for relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S) and other treatable causes that supplements do not address.
