Anxiety: The Evidence-Based Supplement Protocol

7 min read ·
Bottom Line

Ashwagandha, L-theanine, lavender (Silexan), magnesium, and saffron for anxiety: what randomized trials actually show, and what to avoid.

Educational reference, not medical advice. Always consult a clinician before changing your supplement regimen. See our methodology for how scores are derived. Questions or corrections: hello@supplementscore.org.

Supplements do not replace therapy or, for moderate-to-severe anxiety, prescription treatment—and persistent or worsening anxiety warrants a clinician, not a supplement aisle. That said, several have genuine randomized-trial evidence for milder, stress-related anxiety: ashwagandha for stress and cortisol, L-theanine for calm without sedation, the standardized lavender-oil preparation Silexan for generalized anxiety, and magnesium in deficiency-prone people. Saffron has emerging mood data too. Here is what the trials actually support, and what to skip.

Ashwagandha, 240–600 mg Daily

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is the best-supported botanical here. In a 60-day randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of stressed adults, a standardized extract (240 mg/day) significantly reduced Hamilton Anxiety Scale scores and lowered morning cortisol versus placebo, consistent with a calming effect on the stress (HPA) axis. Multiple trials echo reductions in perceived stress and anxiety at 240–600 mg/day of a standardized root extract. Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated; cautions include possible thyroid-hormone elevation, liver-injury case reports, and avoidance in pregnancy. Compare options in our ashwagandha vs L-theanine for stress guide.

L-Theanine, 200 mg Daily

L-theanine, an amino acid from tea, promotes a relaxed-but-alert state without sedation. In a four-week randomized placebo-controlled trial in healthy adults, 200 mg/day of L-theanine reduced anxiety-trait and stress-related symptom scores and improved sleep quality, with some gains in verbal fluency and executive function. The effect size is modest and best suited to everyday stress rather than a diagnosed anxiety disorder, but its safety and lack of dependence make it an easy first trial, including alongside caffeine to blunt the jitters.

Lavender Oil (Silexan), 80–160 mg Daily

The standardized oral lavender-oil preparation Silexan has the strongest disease-level evidence of this group. In a randomized, double-blind trial of 539 adults with generalized anxiety disorder, Silexan (80 or 160 mg/day) was superior to placebo on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale and performed comparably to paroxetine, with adverse-event rates similar to placebo. An earlier trial found it comparable to lorazepam without sedation or dependence. The main side effect is mild eructation (lavender burps). A specific lavender product is not in our database, so we mention it without a link; look for the studied 80–160 mg standardized preparation rather than generic lavender capsules or essential oil.

Magnesium, 200–350 mg Daily (If Intake Is Low)

A systematic review of magnesium and subjective anxiety found suggestive benefit in anxiety-vulnerable groups—mildly anxious adults and women with premenstrual syndrome—though the authors judged the overall evidence quality as poor and called for better trials. The reasonable interpretation: correcting a real magnesium shortfall may help stress and anxiety symptoms, but magnesium is not a stand-alone anxiety drug for the already-replete. Magnesium glycinate is well absorbed and gentle on the gut.

Saffron, 30 mg Daily

Saffron (Crocus sativus) has randomized evidence mostly for depressive symptoms, with smaller signals for anxiety. As an adjunct or for mild low mood with anxious features, standardized saffron at about 30 mg/day is a plausible, well-tolerated option, but the anxiety-specific data are thinner than for ashwagandha or Silexan. Use a standardized extract and reassess after several weeks.

What NOT to Take

Avoid kava for routine use—it can relieve anxiety but carries a real risk of liver injury, so it is not a casual self-prescribed supplement. Be cautious stacking serotonergic supplements (saffron, 5-HTP, St. John's wort) with SSRIs or SNRIs, which risks serotonin syndrome, and St. John's wort interacts with many drugs including contraceptives. Do not use alcohol or high-dose sedating "sleep stacks" to manage daytime anxiety. And do not substitute any supplement for treatment of panic disorder, severe GAD, or anxiety with suicidal thoughts—seek care.

How to Run the Protocol

Pair any supplement with the high-yield basics: regular exercise, sleep, reduced caffeine and alcohol, and—for anything beyond mild stress—evidence-based therapy such as CBT. Add one product at a time. A reasonable starting point is ashwagandha 300–600 mg/day of a standardized extract, or L-theanine 200 mg/day for everyday stress; Silexan 80–160 mg/day is the better-evidenced choice if symptoms meet generalized-anxiety criteria. Correct a documented magnesium shortfall. Give each a fair 4–8 week trial and track symptoms; if it does not help, stop rather than stacking. Tell your clinician what you are taking, especially if you take antidepressants, sedatives, or thyroid medication.
