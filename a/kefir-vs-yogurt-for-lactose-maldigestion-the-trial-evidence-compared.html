Kefir vs Yogurt for Lactose Maldigestion: The Trial Evidence Compared

6 min read · By the SupplementScore team · Updated 15 October 2026 · How we research
Bottom Line

In a 2003 trial of 15 adults with lactose maldigestion, plain kefir and plain yogurt both produced much less breath hydrogen than milk after a 20 g lactose serving (about 87 and 76 versus 224 ppm·h). Flavored kefir sat in between. Kefir was not clearly better than yogurt. Flatulence was about 54% to 71% milder with the fermented milks than with milk. Pasteurized products were not the ones tested. Lactase tablets remain the flexible option for ordinary milk or ice cream. This does not apply to cow's milk protein allergy.

Lactose maldigestion is one of the most common GI conditions on earth — roughly two-thirds of adults globally retain low intestinal lactase activity past childhood. The classic clinical observation, formalized in trials going back to the 1980s, is that fermented dairy is better tolerated than fresh milk by lactose maldigesters. The head-to-head evidence is a 2003 trial, not a 2024 study.

The mechanism: bacterial lactase, not human lactase

Both yogurt and kefir contain live bacteria that produce their own beta-galactosidase. When consumed, the bacteria survive transit and continue hydrolyzing lactose in the small intestine on the consumer's behalf. The classic Kolars 1984 hydrogen-breath-test study showed that lactose maldigesters who drank 18 g lactose as yogurt produced about a third the breath hydrogen of those who drank the same dose as milk. Symptoms tracked the hydrogen.

Kefir is fermented with a more diverse microbial community than yogurt — typically Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens, multiple lactobacilli, Acetobacter, and several yeasts including Saccharomyces and Kluyveromyces species. The yeasts in particular bring substantial beta-galactosidase activity, and they may also continue fermenting in the small intestine.

What the 2003 trial measured

Hertzler and Clancy tested 15 adults with lactose maldigestion. Each person drank 20 g lactose servings of 2% milk, plain kefir, flavored kefir, plain yogurt, and flavored yogurt, then had breath hydrogen measured for 8 hours (PMID 12728216).

Plain yogurt and plain kefir were similar, and both were far below milk: breath-hydrogen area was about 76 ppm·h for plain yogurt, 87 for plain kefir, and 224 for milk. Flavored kefir was higher, about 156. Yogurt and kefir reduced the severity of flatulence by 54% to 71% compared with milk. Abdominal pain and diarrhea were negligible across the drinks. Kefir did not beat yogurt.

Where the differences narrow

Several caveats matter for translation. Pasteurized kefir — the long shelf-life version sold in some markets — loses most of its live bacteria and yeast and behaves more like milk in maldigesters. Heat treatment kills the very microbes that deliver the lactase. Similarly, heat-treated yogurt loses its advantage. The 2003 trial used refrigerated kefir and yogurt, not heat-treated products.

Kefir grains vary considerably between commercial producers; lab analyses of commercial kefirs have found large variability in actual lactose content, live bacterial counts, and beta-galactosidase activity. The label rarely tells you what is inside. In the trial that compared them directly, plain kefir did not beat plain yogurt.

Practical takeaway

If you have lactose maldigestion symptoms and want to keep dairy in your diet, plain refrigerated kefir and plain yogurt were both much better tolerated than milk, and kefir was not superior to yogurt. Pasteurized shelf-stable kefir or yogurt offers little advantage over milk. Lactase enzyme tablets remain the most reliable per-dose option when you want to consume regular milk or ice cream; the comparison there is symptom magnitude (fermented dairy wins) versus dosing flexibility (lactase tablets win).

Note that lactose maldigestion and milk protein allergy are different conditions; nothing in this analysis applies to IgE-mediated cow's milk allergy.
