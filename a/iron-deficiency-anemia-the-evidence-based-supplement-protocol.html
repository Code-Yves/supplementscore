Iron-Deficiency Anemia: The Evidence-Based Supplement Protocol

7 min read ·
Bottom Line

Ferrous bisglycinate, alternate-day dosing, vitamin C, and lactoferrin — the evidence-based way to replete iron with the fewest side effects.

Iron-deficiency anemia (IDA) is the one supplement scenario where the "supplement" is the treatment, not an adjunct — but only after the cause of the iron loss is found (menstrual, GI bleeding, malabsorption, or inadequate intake). The goal is to replace iron with the fewest side effects and the most absorption. The evidence has shifted in two important ways: gentler iron salts such as ferrous bisglycinate match traditional ferrous sulfate at lower elemental doses, and alternate-day dosing now appears to absorb better than splitting iron through the day. Vitamin C and lactoferrin round out the toolkit. Here is the evidence-based way to run repletion.

Oral Iron, ~60–120 mg Elemental Every Other Day

The landmark physiology comes from two open-label RCTs in iron-depleted women: giving iron on consecutive days raised the iron-regulating hormone hepcidin and lowered fractional absorption from the next dose, whereas alternate-day, single-morning dosing produced higher cumulative absorption. Twice-daily split dosing was actually worse than once daily. The practical translation: a single morning dose of roughly 60–120 mg elemental iron every other day often delivers as much absorbed iron as daily dosing with fewer GI side effects. Iron repletion is a marathon — hemoglobin starts rising in 2–4 weeks, but you continue for 3–6 months after hemoglobin normalizes to refill ferritin stores.

Iron Form: Ferrous Bisglycinate vs Ferrous Sulfate

Traditional ferrous sulfate is effective and cheap but its high elemental load drives nausea, cramping, and constipation, which wreck adherence. Ferrous bisglycinate is a chelated form that is well absorbed at lower elemental doses and is typically better tolerated, which matters because the best iron salt is the one a patient will actually keep taking. Ferrous gluconate is an intermediate option. The choice is mostly about tolerability, not a large efficacy gap — see our ferrous bisglycinate vs sulfate comparison and the iron forms guide.

Vitamin C — Helpful as a Co-Factor, Not a Requirement

Ascorbic acid keeps iron in its more-absorbable ferrous state and can increase non-heme iron uptake from a given meal, which is why iron is classically taken with orange juice or a vitamin C tablet. The real-world effect on repletion is modest, though — some trials find that once you are dosing iron correctly, added vitamin C adds little to hemoglobin recovery. Treat it as a cheap, low-risk co-factor rather than a must. Far more important is avoiding absorption blockers at the same time: separate iron from coffee, tea, calcium, and antacids/PPIs by a couple of hours.

Lactoferrin, ~100–250 mg Daily (Tolerability Alternative)

Lactoferrin, an iron-binding milk glycoprotein, is an option when oral iron salts are intolerable. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials found oral bovine lactoferrin raised hemoglobin at least as much as ferrous sulfate in low-hemoglobin patients, with fewer GI side effects and better adherence. It is more expensive and the trial quality is mixed, so it is a second-line tolerability play rather than a routine first choice — but a reasonable one for pregnant patients or anyone who cannot stomach conventional iron.

What NOT to Take

Do not start iron without confirming deficiency (low ferritin) and pursuing the cause — treating anemia of chronic disease or, worse, undiagnosed GI cancer with iron is a serious miss. Avoid twice-daily split dosing and very high single doses; both raise hepcidin and reduce net absorption while increasing side effects. Do not take iron with calcium, coffee, tea, or PPIs. Never give adult iron supplements where young children can reach them — iron overdose is a leading cause of pediatric poisoning deaths. And do not keep self-supplementing indefinitely once stores are full: iron overload is harmful, especially in men and in hereditary hemochromatosis.

How to Run the Protocol

Confirm IDA with ferritin and a cause work-up, then start ~60–120 mg elemental iron (ferrous bisglycinate if tolerability is a concern) as a single morning dose every other day, with or without a vitamin C source. Keep it away from coffee, tea, dairy, and acid-suppressing drugs. Recheck hemoglobin and reticulocytes at 2–4 weeks and ferritin at ~3 months; continue 3–6 months past hemoglobin normalization to rebuild stores. If oral iron is intolerable or absorption is poor (e.g., celiac disease, prior bariatric surgery), discuss lactoferrin or IV iron with your clinician rather than pushing the oral dose higher.
