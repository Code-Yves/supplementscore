Garlic and Perioperative Bleeding: Why Surgeons Want Patients to Stop Two Weeks Out

May 14, 2026 · 4 min read · By the SupplementScore Editorial Team · Reviewed against our evidence methodology

Most patients answer "no herbal supplements" honestly when asked at preoperative assessment because they do not consider garlic capsules or fish oil to count. A standardized garlic supplement can produce a measurable platelet effect, and the perioperative herbal guidance issued by major anesthesia societies lists garlic among the agents to stop at least 7 days — and ideally 14 — before elective surgery. The risk is real but contextual.

The platelet pharmacology of garlic

Aged garlic extract and fresh-crushed garlic contain ajoene and related organosulfur compounds that inhibit platelet aggregation in vitro through cyclooxygenase pathways, intracellular calcium signaling, and direct effects on the fibrinogen receptor. Effect sizes in human ex vivo studies vary widely and depend on preparation (fresh vs aged, raw vs cooked), allicin content, and dose. The clinical significance of these in vitro effects has been debated for decades but case reports of postoperative bleeding link garlic supplementation to spontaneous spinal epidural hematoma and increased operative bleeding in selected patients [1].

The American Society of Anesthesiologists position

The ASA preoperative herbal medicines guidance, originating from Ang-Lee and colleagues' 2001 JAMA review, lists garlic alongside ginkgo, ginseng, ephedra, kava, and St. John's wort as herbs to discontinue before surgery [2]. The recommended washout for garlic is at least 7 days. The clinical rationale balances the modest individual risk against the cumulative perioperative risk of dual antiplatelet effects when patients are simultaneously on aspirin or undergoing procedures with high bleeding consequences (neurosurgery, ophthalmologic surgery, cardiac surgery).

The actual outcome evidence

Randomized cardiovascular outcome data showing increased clinical bleeding from garlic at typical supplement doses are limited. The Garlic and Stents trial in patients on aspirin and clopidogrel did not show meaningful additive bleeding with garlic 600 mg/day, although it was not powered for hard outcomes [3]. The bleeding case reports — including the spontaneous spinal hematoma case reported in Neurosurgery 1990 — involve unusually high or high-dose preparations, often combined with antiplatelet drugs. The probability of clinically significant bleeding from low-dose dietary garlic alone is low; the prudent approach for elective surgery is still to discontinue.

Concurrent risk for medications

Garlic interactions are not limited to surgery. Garlic supplements have been shown to reduce plasma concentrations of the protease inhibitor saquinavir significantly through CYP3A4 induction [4], and may interact with warfarin, increasing INR variability. The most consistent evidence is for the warfarin interaction, where case reports document elevated INR and bleeding episodes [5]. Patients on warfarin should not start or stop garlic supplements without an INR check at 1-2 weeks.

What level of intake matters

Dietary garlic in food — a few cloves a week in cooking — has not been associated with clinically significant antiplatelet effects in trials. Standardized supplements at 600-900 mg/day of dried powder or 4,000 mcg allicin equivalent are where the documented effects concentrate. Aged garlic extract products at 1,200-2,400 mg/day are similarly worth disclosing. The relevant question for any preoperative patient is not "do you take garlic?" but "do you take a garlic capsule or aged garlic extract product?"

The preoperative checklist worth remembering

Herbs commonly associated with bleeding risk that should be stopped 7-14 days before surgery include garlic, ginkgo, ginger (at supplement doses), high-dose fish oil, vitamin E above 400 IU/day, feverfew, and turmeric supplements at high doses. None individually causes catastrophic bleeding at typical doses in low-risk patients, but the cumulative effect in patients on aspirin, anticoagulants, or undergoing high-risk procedures is real. Tell the surgeon what you actually take, including herbs — the question is on every pre-op form for a reason.
