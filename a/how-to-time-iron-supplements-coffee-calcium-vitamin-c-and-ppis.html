How to time iron supplements: coffee, calcium, vitamin C, and PPIs

May 18, 2026 · 5 min read · By the SupplementScore Editorial Team · Reviewed against our evidence methodology

Oral iron is one of the most poorly absorbed supplements in routine clinical use, with bioavailability ranging from about 2% to 25% depending on iron status, the form ingested, and what else is in the stomach at the same time. A handful of timing rules account for most of the practical difference between treatment success and stubborn iron deficiency. This is a guide to those rules.

The alternate-day rule

Standard advice was once daily or twice-daily iron dosing. Stable isotope studies from the Zurich group changed that. Moretti and colleagues showed that an oral iron dose acutely raises serum hepcidin for 24-36 hours, which then suppresses absorption of the next dose taken within that window [1]. A follow-up randomized trial demonstrated that alternate-day dosing produced higher cumulative iron absorption than the same total dose given daily, and a 2020 confirmatory trial showed alternate-day dosing also produced equivalent or greater rises in hemoglobin in iron-deficient women with fewer GI side effects [2].

Vitamin C: a real effect, but smaller than the textbooks say

Vitamin C reduces dietary ferric iron to the absorbable ferrous form and chelates inhibitors such as polyphenols and phytate. Classical isotope work showed that 100 mg of ascorbic acid taken with iron roughly doubled non-heme iron absorption in low-iron meals [3]. The effect is dose-dependent: 25-50 mg adds little; 100-200 mg is the practical maximum. A 2020 randomized trial in 440 women with iron-deficiency anemia found no difference in hemoglobin response between iron alone and iron plus 200 mg vitamin C over 8 weeks, suggesting that for therapeutic doses of supplemental iron, the relative bioavailability boost matters less than for dietary iron [4].

Coffee, tea, and the polyphenol problem

The Hallberg group documented in the 1980s that coffee taken with a meal reduced iron absorption by 60% and tea by 75-94%, mediated by polyphenols binding to iron in the gut lumen and forming insoluble complexes [5]. The effect is meal-specific: coffee taken one hour before a meal had no measurable effect on iron absorption, while coffee taken with the meal or up to one hour after did. The practical rule that follows is a 2-hour gap between iron and coffee or tea on either side, especially for non-heme iron supplements and plant-based meals.

Calcium: real but modest interference

Calcium reduces non-heme iron absorption by 30-50% at single doses of 300-600 mg taken with iron [6]. The effect is dose-dependent and saturable, and unlike the polyphenol effect it does not persist with long-term coadministration; the body partially compensates over weeks. Spacing iron and calcium supplements by 2 hours, and avoiding milk or fortified dairy products at the time iron is taken, is sufficient to neutralize most of the effect.

Acid suppression and the PPI problem

Iron requires gastric acid for the conversion of ferric to ferrous forms and for solubilization. Long-term proton-pump inhibitor use roughly doubles the risk of iron deficiency in cohort data [7]. The 2024 American Gastroenterological Association clinical practice update suggests checking ferritin annually in patients on chronic PPI therapy and considering ferrous fumarate or sulfate taken at least 4 hours apart from the PPI dose [8]. Liquid forms and bisglycinate formulations may be modestly better absorbed in the achlorhydric state than enteric-coated tablets.

Bottom line

The highest-yield timing rules for oral iron are alternate-day dosing, taking iron on an empty stomach when tolerated, separating iron from coffee, tea, dairy, and calcium supplements by 2 hours, and pairing each dose with 100 mg of vitamin C from food or supplement. PPI users need a 4-hour separation and periodic ferritin monitoring.
