Functional Dyspepsia: The Evidence-Based Supplement Protocol

7 min read ·
Bottom Line

Peppermint-caraway oil, STW 5 (Iberogast), and ginger — the supplements with real randomized-trial evidence for functional dyspepsia, and what to skip.

Functional dyspepsia (FD) — chronic upper-abdominal fullness, early satiety, and burning with no ulcer or structural cause — is driven by impaired gastric accommodation, delayed emptying, and visceral hypersensitivity. First-line care is a Helicobacter pylori test-and-treat, a trial of a proton-pump inhibitor, and, for many, a low-dose neuromodulator. A small number of botanicals have genuine randomized-trial support as add-ons: a fixed peppermint–caraway oil combination, the multi-herb formula STW 5 (Iberogast), and ginger for the delayed-emptying phenotype. Here is what the trials actually show, and what to skip.

Peppermint + Caraway Oil, ~90 mg + 50 mg Twice Daily

The best-evidenced supplement for FD is the fixed combination of peppermint oil and caraway oil (marketed as Menthacarin). A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials found it significantly reduced epigastric pain intensity (standardized mean difference ~0.80) and improved global symptom scores versus placebo, with effects comparable to a reference prokinetic. Mechanistically, peppermint oil relaxes gastric smooth muscle and blunts visceral hypersensitivity while caraway reduces spasm and bloating. The main side effect is reflux or a peppermint "burp"; an enteric-coated formulation reduces this. It is the first supplement worth trying for the pain-predominant (epigastric pain syndrome) phenotype.

STW 5 (Iberogast), 20 Drops Three Times Daily

STW 5 is a standardized blend of nine herbal extracts (including bitter candytuft, peppermint, chamomile, and licorice) that acts on several gastric targets at once — relaxing the fundus, tonicizing the antrum, and reducing acid and hypersensitivity. A pooled meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials found it more effective than placebo for the most bothersome GI symptom (odds ratio ~0.22), and a separate trial showed no significant difference versus the prokinetic cisapride. Tolerability is good, with adverse-event rates near placebo. A regulatory caution: rare hepatotoxicity has been reported with STW 5, so it is not for anyone with liver disease, and you should stop it if you develop jaundice or dark urine. The licorice component also argues against open-ended use in people with hypertension.

Ginger, 1–1.2 g Daily (Postprandial-Distress Phenotype)

Ginger accelerates gastric emptying and stimulates antral contractions, which makes it most rational for the postprandial-distress (fullness and early-satiety) phenotype rather than the pain phenotype. Crossover studies in healthy volunteers and FD patients show ginger speeds emptying and reduces fullness after a test meal; symptom RCTs are smaller and shorter than for peppermint–caraway, so treat ginger as a reasonable, low-risk adjunct rather than a proven monotherapy. It pairs naturally with the nausea component many FD patients also report. Doses above ~3–4 g/day add little and can themselves cause heartburn.

Artichoke Leaf and Others — Weaker, Conditional

Artichoke leaf extract has positive but lower-quality trials in functional dyspepsia and overlapping biliary-type dyspepsia, working partly through a choleretic (bile-stimulating) effect; it is not in our database, so we mention it without a rating — reasonable to try if peppermint–caraway and STW 5 disappoint, but the evidence base is thin. Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) is popular for "stomach soothing" but has little controlled FD-specific evidence. If reflux overlaps with your symptoms, melatonin has small trials for functional heartburn, but it is not an FD treatment per se.

What NOT to Take

Do not use peppermint oil if reflux or a hiatal hernia dominates — it relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter and can worsen heartburn. Avoid STW 5 with existing liver disease given the rare hepatotoxicity signal, and avoid licorice-containing products if you have hypertension or take a thiazide/loop diuretic (pseudohyperaldosteronism risk). Skip high-dose ginger if you are on a blood thinner without clinician sign-off. And do not substitute any of these for an H. pylori work-up or alarm-feature evaluation — weight loss, dysphagia, GI bleeding, or new dyspepsia after age 60 needs endoscopy, not a supplement.

How to Run the Protocol

Match the supplement to your phenotype. For epigastric pain, start peppermint–caraway oil twice daily, or STW 5 at 20 drops three times daily; give either a 4-week trial. For postprandial fullness and early satiety, add ginger 1–1.2 g/day before meals. Keep a PPI trial and, where appropriate, a neuromodulator as the medical backbone — supplements are adjuncts. Re-evaluate at 4 weeks: if symptoms have not meaningfully improved, stop the supplement rather than stacking more, and revisit the diagnosis (including gastroparesis and rumination) with your clinician. Stopping caffeine, alcohol, and large fatty meals often does as much as any capsule.
