Chronic Constipation: The Evidence-Based Supplement Protocol

7 min read ·
Bottom Line

Psyllium, magnesium, probiotics, and green kiwifruit for chronic constipation: what randomized trials actually support, and what to skip.

Educational reference, not medical advice. Always consult a clinician before changing your supplement regimen. See our methodology for how scores are derived. Questions or corrections: hello@supplementscore.org.

Most chronic constipation responds to a few well-evidenced, low-cost measures before anyone needs a prescription. The supplements with real randomized-trial support are psyllium (the best-studied soluble fiber), magnesium as an osmotic agent, probiotics (modest, strain-specific), and whole green kiwifruit. Adequate fluids and movement matter too. Red-flag symptoms—blood in stool, unintended weight loss, new constipation after age 50—need a clinician, not a supplement. Here is what the trials actually support, and what to skip.

Psyllium, 10–20 g Daily (in Divided Doses)

Fiber is the evidence-based first step, and psyllium is the standout. An updated systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials found fiber supplementation improved constipation, with psyllium specifically showing significant effects on response rate, stool frequency, and consistency—benefits clearest at doses above 10 g/day and durations of at least four weeks. Psyllium is a soluble, gel-forming fiber that holds water in stool. Start low (3–5 g) and build up with plenty of water to limit gas and bloating, which are the main side effects. See our psyllium vs inulin comparison for how soluble fibers differ.

Magnesium, 200–500 mg Daily (Citrate or Oxide)

Magnesium draws water into the bowel osmotically, softening stool and prompting movement. In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial in adults with chronic constipation, magnesium oxide (1.5 g/day) improved overall symptoms, bowel-movement frequency, and quality of life to a degree comparable with the stimulant laxative senna, and with no severe adverse events. Magnesium citrate and oxide are both effective; citrate is generally better absorbed and gentler. Reduce the dose if stools become loose, and use caution if you have kidney impairment, since magnesium can accumulate. See magnesium citrate vs oxide for differences.

Probiotics — Modest and Strain-Specific

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials (1,182 adults) found probiotics reduced whole-gut transit time by about 12 hours and increased stool frequency by roughly 1.3 bowel movements per week, with the clearest effect for Bifidobacterium lactis; Lactobacillus casei Shirota did not significantly help. The authors cautioned that heterogeneity and bias were high. Translation: probiotics are a reasonable, low-risk add-on, but pick a Bifidobacterium-containing product, give it 4 weeks, and do not expect a dramatic effect. The proposed mechanism is a shift in gut microbial fermentation and stool water content rather than a direct laxative push, which is partly why effects build gradually and vary so much between strains and individuals.

Green Kiwifruit, 2 Fruit Daily

Whole green kiwifruit is a genuinely evidence-based food intervention. In an international multicenter randomized crossover trial, eating two green kiwifruits daily for four weeks produced a clinically relevant increase of at least 1.5 complete spontaneous bowel movements per week in people with functional constipation and constipation-predominant IBS, and improved abdominal comfort—with no significant adverse events. Kiwifruit is not in our supplement database, so we mention it without a link, but it is a well-tolerated first thing to try, and unlike many fiber supplements it tends not to increase bloating.

What NOT to Take

Do not rely long-term on stimulant laxatives sold as "natural" cleanses—senna, cascara, and aloe latex—for daily use without medical guidance; they can cause cramping and dependence-like patterns. Avoid stacking multiple osmotic and stimulant products at once, which risks diarrhea, dehydration, and electrolyte disturbance. Be cautious with high-dose magnesium if you have reduced kidney function. And do not treat new or alarm-feature constipation (bleeding, weight loss, anemia, onset after 50) with supplements—get evaluated to rule out serious causes.

How to Run the Protocol

Start with the basics that the evidence rewards: increase fluids, move daily, and add fiber. A practical sequence is psyllium titrated up to 10–20 g/day with water over a week or two; if that is not enough, add magnesium citrate or oxide (200–500 mg/day), adjusting to keep stools soft but not loose. Two green kiwifruits a day is an easy, well-tolerated addition, and a Bifidobacterium probiotic is a reasonable trial for 4 weeks. Give each step time—fiber and probiotics work over weeks, not days. If you are still constipated after a fair trial, or if alarm symptoms appear, see a clinician before escalating.
