The Iron-Deficiency Recovery Stack: Iron, Vitamin C, Lactoferrin, and B12

6 min read ·

Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world and one of the most poorly managed in primary care. The current evidence-based approach is not "65 mg of ferrous sulfate three times daily" — that protocol fails because hepcidin upregulation after the first dose blocks absorption of the next several. The Stoffel trials in Lancet Haematology rewrote this in 2017–2020. A practical recovery stack uses four components: alternate-day high-dose ferrous bisglycinate, vitamin C to enhance non-heme iron absorption, lactoferrin as an alternative or adjunct for adults who cannot tolerate oral iron, and B12 if co-deficient. None of this addresses the underlying cause of iron loss — that requires medical workup.

Layer 1: Alternate-Day Ferrous Bisglycinate, 60–100 mg Elemental

The Stoffel et al. 2017 trial in iron-depleted young women demonstrated that splitting iron doses across the day reduced fractional absorption due to hepcidin elevation, and that single morning doses on alternate days produced higher cumulative absorption than daily or twice-daily dosing. The same group's 2020 follow-up extended this to twice-weekly dosing as a maintenance strategy. Ferrous bisglycinate is generally better-tolerated than sulfate at equivalent elemental doses and has comparable or slightly better fractional absorption. Take fasted with vitamin C if possible; if GI tolerability requires it, with a small meal. Re-check ferritin and transferrin saturation at 8–12 weeks. See our iron dosing guide.

Layer 2: Vitamin C, 100–200 mg With Each Iron Dose

Ascorbic acid converts non-heme ferric iron (Fe³⁺) to the more absorbable ferrous form (Fe²⁺) in the duodenum and forms a soluble chelate that is less affected by phytates and polyphenols. Stable-isotope studies have shown that 100 mg of ascorbic acid taken simultaneously with non-heme iron can double or triple fractional absorption. A 2020 RCT in iron-deficient adults questioned whether the absorption boost translates to hemoglobin gains over 12 weeks — the answer was modest at best — but the cost is negligible and the mechanism is well-established. Take 200 mg of vitamin C with your alternate-day iron dose. See the vitamin C dossier.

Layer 3: Lactoferrin, 100–200 mg Daily (Tolerability Alternative)

Lactoferrin is an iron-binding glycoprotein from bovine milk that has emerged as a non-traditional oral iron repletion approach. Multiple RCTs in pregnancy-associated and post-bariatric iron deficiency have shown that bovine lactoferrin 100–200 mg daily produces hemoglobin and ferritin gains comparable to ferrous sulfate with substantially fewer GI side effects. The mechanism appears to combine direct iron delivery via the lactoferrin-LfR pathway and modulation of intestinal hepcidin. It is more expensive than ferrous iron but is the most useful option for adults who cannot tolerate oral iron and would otherwise require IV therapy. See our lactoferrin review.

Layer 4: Vitamin B12 — Only If Co-Deficient (1,000 mcg Daily Oral or 1,000 mcg IM Weekly)

Iron deficiency and B12 deficiency often coexist, particularly in vegetarians and adults over 65 with reduced gastric acid (atrophic gastritis, chronic PPI use). They can mimic each other clinically — both produce fatigue and pallor — and one can mask the macrocytosis or microcytosis the other should produce. Check serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and holotranscobalamin if available. If deficient, supplement 1,000 mcg cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin daily orally for repletion (oral high-dose is non-inferior to IM in most cases — the Cochrane review confirmed this in 2018). See our B12 form review and the iron deficiency anemia condition page.

What NOT to Add

Avoid taking iron with calcium, dairy, tea, coffee, or phytate-rich whole grains — all of these significantly reduce non-heme iron absorption. "Heme iron polypeptide" supplements are marketed as superior but the trial evidence is thin and the cost is high. Skip "blood-building" herbal blends entirely — yellow dock, nettle, and chlorophyll have no controlled evidence for treating iron deficiency anemia. Do not start iron without confirming deficiency with ferritin (and CRP if inflammation is suspected, since ferritin is an acute-phase reactant); supplementing iron in a non-deficient adult is potentially harmful — see our iron overload piece.

How to Run the Stack and Bottom Line

Confirm deficiency with ferritin (and an etiologic workup if low). Start alternate-day ferrous bisglycinate 60–100 mg elemental + vitamin C 200 mg, morning. Re-test at 8–12 weeks. If GI tolerability fails, swap to lactoferrin 100–200 mg daily. Co-test and supplement B12 if indicated. Restoring ferritin from 10 → 50 ng/mL typically takes 12–24 weeks. Iron deficiency does not generally need IV therapy unless oral fails or absorption is impaired (IBD, post-bariatric, celiac).
