Bovine colostrum for athlete immunity: what the 2024-2025 trials measured

6 min read · By the SupplementScore team · Updated 15 October 2026 · How we research
Bottom Line

Bovine colostrum — the antibody-rich first milk cows produce after calving — has been tested in athletes for respiratory illness, not as a proven performance aid. A 2016 review of five trials (152 adults) found 38% fewer upper-respiratory episodes and 44% fewer symptom-days over 8–12 weeks than placebo. Four of those five trials were judged at moderate or high risk of bias. Doses in the trials on this page run from 10 to 60 g/day, mixed with cold liquid because heat can damage the proteins. A 2002 study of nine men taking 60 g/day for four weeks found no change in blood IGF-1. Tested athletes should still check with their anti-doping program before using it.

Bovine colostrum is a concentrated source of immunoglobulins, lactoferrin, and growth factors, which is why endurance athletes have used it since the late 1990s to blunt the dip in mucosal immunity that follows heavy training. Two decades of trials have produced a moderately consistent signal on illness days, not on race performance.

The mucosal immunity hypothesis

The mechanistic case rests on the "open window" model of post-exercise immunosuppression, in which secretory IgA in saliva falls for several hours after prolonged endurance exercise. A 2007 trial in 29 highly trained cyclists gave 10 g/day of bovine colostrum protein or whey for five weeks, then a block of hard training. Colostrum changed several blood immune markers and showed a trend toward fewer upper-respiratory symptoms (P = 0.055). It did not show that a 20 g dose preserves salivary IgA.1

What the early meta-analyses showed

A 2016 review of five trials (152 adults who train) found colostrum cut the rate of upper-respiratory episodes by 38% (rate ratio 0.62) and symptom-days by 44% (rate ratio 0.56) over 8–12 weeks. Four of the five trials were judged at moderate or high risk of bias because the methods were poorly reported.2 In a separate study of 35 distance runners, 12 weeks of colostrum raised median salivary IgA by 79%. The authors said the result should be read cautiously because the group was small and IgA levels varied widely. That study did not show a clear drop in infections.3

Immune markers versus illness

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 randomised trials in athletes and physically active people, however, found that the signal sits mainly on illness outcomes rather than systemic immunity: bovine colostrum had no or only a fairly low effect on circulating and salivary immunoglobulins, lymphocytes, and neutrophils, even though earlier work has shown reduced upper respiratory infections (PMID: 32276466).4

What colostrum does not do

Performance results are mixed, and this page does not cite a review that found a consistent null. In one trial, 35 elite field hockey players took 60 g/day of colostrum or whey for 8 weeks. The colostrum group improved a 5×10 m sprint more than the whey group. Jump height, body composition, and the endurance tests did not differ.6

The IGF-1 and doping considerations

Colostrum naturally contains insulin-like growth factor 1. In a 2002 study, nine endurance-trained men took 60 g/day for 4 weeks. Blood IGF-1 was about 31 nmol/L before and 33 nmol/L after, and an Olympic-accredited lab found no banned substance.7 Tested athletes should still check with their anti-doping program before using it.

Dose, timing, and practical positioning

The trials above used about 10 to 60 g/day, almost always as a powder mixed with cold water or food. Heat can denature the proteins, so colostrum should not be added to hot drinks. The illness finding comes from adults who were training, and four of the five trials in the 2016 review had important reporting gaps. This is not a reason for a non-athlete to take colostrum every day.
