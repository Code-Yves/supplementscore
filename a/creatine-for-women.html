Creatine for women, including over 40 and 50

By the SupplementScore team · Updated 21 October 2026 · How we research
Bottom Line

For women after menopause, creatine monohydrate has one clear job: a small lean-mass and leg-strength gain when the dose is at least 5 g a day and you lift. A 2026 review of 7 trials found +0.37 kg of lean mass and +7.5 kg on the leg press, and no bone-density change. A 69-trial strength review found several of those strength gains in men and not in the female subgroup. Skip it as a belly-fat or "hormone" product.

Educational reference, not medical advice. This page cites only studies we read. It does not replace a clinician, especially if you are pregnant, have kidney disease, or already take a prescription.

Creatine monohydrate is sold to women as a toning powder, a menopause fix, and sometimes as a fat-loss product. The trials do not support that marketing. What they support is narrower: a small lean-mass and leg-strength effect in postmenopausal women when the dose is at least 5 g a day and they are also lifting weights. A large strength review that split results by sex did not find the same significant strength gains in women as in men.

Over 40 and after menopause

The women-only pooled estimate we read is Naddafha and colleagues, 2026 (PMID 42141930, doi:10.1080/15502783.2026.2668435). They pooled randomized, placebo-controlled trials in postmenopausal women (mean age about 62; the eligibility window started around 40 to 45). Seven trials, 608 people randomized, lasting 12 to 104 weeks (median 38).

  • Lean mass, 5 trials, 338 people: mean difference +0.37 kg (95% CI +0.05 to +0.69).
  • Leg-press one-rep max, 3 trials, 111 people: mean difference +7.5 kg (95% CI +2.2 to +12.8).
  • Those gains showed up when creatine was at least 5 g/day and combined with resistance training. Trials at 3 g/day or less, without resistance training, showed no measurable effect.
  • Bone density was unchanged overall. The authors say any bone effect remains unclear.
  • Adverse events were mild and similar to placebo. Renal indices were unchanged in these trials.

That is a real signal. It is also small. A third of a kilogram of lean mass is not a body recomposition program, and 111 people on the strength outcome is a thin evidence base. The review was not prospectively registered. One author chairs a creatine advisory board supported by a creatine manufacturer, and the journal's society receives money from companies that sell creatine.

An earlier meta-analysis in older adults of both sexes (Chilibeck 2017, PMID 29138605) is larger and more optimistic, and it is not a women-only result. Twenty-two trials, 721 men and women, mean ages 57 to 70 across studies, resistance training 2 to 3 days a week for 7 to 52 weeks. Creatine added 1.37 kg of lean tissue (95% CI 0.97 to 1.76), with smaller standardized gains in chest press and leg press. Use that figure for mixed older groups. Do not paste it onto women over 50 as if the 2026 women-only review had found the same thing.

Younger women, and the male-heavy trials

Kazeminasab and colleagues, 2025 (PMID 40944139, doi:10.3390/nu17172748), pooled 69 studies and 1,937 adults. Creatine plus resistance training beat placebo by 1.43 kg on bench or chest press and 5.64 kg on squat, with smaller gains in vertical jump and Wingate peak power. Handgrip and leg press were not significant overall. When they split by sex, leg press, squat, vertical jump, and Wingate peak power rose in males and were not significant in females. The authors say women and older adults are under-represented and need better trials. One author has advised companies that sell creatine.

A 2002 meta-analysis (Dempsey, PMID 12485548) is mostly of historical interest. Sixteen trials. Bench press was 6.85 kg higher and squats 9.76 kg higher with creatine than placebo, and 7 of 10 maximal-lift studies were young men under 36 who were lifting. The authors wrote that there was no evidence then for women or older adults, and that safety was unproven. Later trials filled part of that gap. They did not turn creatine into a supplement every woman in her 20s or 30s needs.

What this is not

Nothing we read tested creatine as a treatment for belly fat, cycle symptoms, or hair. Bone density was the menopause outcome people hope for, and the pooled postmenopausal trials did not show a change. The practical dose in the positive postmenopausal trials was at least 5 g of creatine monohydrate a day, with resistance training, not a scoop in a coffee as a substitute for lifting.

People with known kidney disease were not the population in these trials. A lab slip can also misread creatine as kidney damage because creatinine rises when you take it. That question is covered in the side-effects article.

Questions people ask

Does creatine work for women over 40 or 50?

In postmenopausal women, a 2026 review of 7 trials (608 randomized, mean age about 62) found about 0.37 kg more lean mass and about 7.5 kg more leg-press strength when creatine was at least 5 g/day and combined with resistance training. Bone density did not change. A broader strength review did not find the same significant strength gains in its female subgroup as in men.

Should women in their 20s or 30s take creatine?

Only if they are doing the kind of training creatine has been tested with, and they want a possible strength or lean-mass effect. The female subgroup of a 69-trial strength review was not significant for several lifts that were significant in men. Creatine is not a general wellness pill for women who do not resistance train.

Will creatine help menopause weight gain?

The postmenopausal review measured lean mass, strength, and bone density, not belly fat or scale weight as a fat-loss outcome. The lean-mass difference was 0.37 kg. That is not a weight-loss result.
